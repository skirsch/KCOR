# Critical Review of the KCOR Manuscript

## Overall assessment

**My view: KCOR addresses a real statistical problem, and its mathematical core is correct. However, this version of the manuscript still contains validation gaps and several inconsistencies substantial enough to warrant major revisions.** I find the approach interesting as an exploratory method for normalizing cohort comparisons. I would not yet regard it as a method whose general reliability has been demonstrated.

The central issue is this: **knowing how to remove curvature generated by a frailty model does not establish that the curvature observed in the data was actually generated by that frailty.** The manuscript acknowledges this difficulty in several passages, but sometimes gives its diagnostic procedures more power to resolve it than has been demonstrated.

## 1. What I consider sound

### The underlying problem is real

When a cohort contains people with very different mortality risks, the frailest individuals tend to die earlier. The composition of the surviving population therefore changes progressively. Two cohorts can develop different mortality trajectories simply because their initial heterogeneity differs.

This survivor-selection mechanism is well established in the frailty literature. The difficulties it creates for interpreting instantaneous hazard ratios are also well known. **KCOR therefore addresses a legitimate problem.** [1, 2]

The manuscript's potential contribution lies mainly in its specific procedure: estimating depletion geometry from selected periods, accounting for intervening waves, and then constructing a comparison on the transformed scale.

### Gamma-frailty inversion is correct under its assumptions

Simplifying the subscripts, the model assumes:

$$
h_i(t)=z_i h_0(t),
$$

where $z_i$ represents individual frailty, with mean 1 and variance $\theta$.

If this frailty follows a gamma distribution, then:

$$
H_{\mathrm{obs}}(t)
=
\frac{\log\left[1+\theta H_0(t)\right]}{\theta}.
$$

The proposed inversion is consequently correct:

$$
H_0(t)
=
\frac{\exp\left[\theta H_{\mathrm{obs}}(t)\right]-1}{\theta}.
$$

This is the core of **pages 9–10**. If the model is appropriate and $\theta$ is correctly estimated, the transformation does remove the distortion that survivor selection produces within that model.

**The main difficulties concern the estimation and interpretation of $\theta$, followed by the extension to NPH wave periods.**

### Several methodological choices are reasonable

I consider cohorts defined at a common entry date, prespecified estimation windows, sensitivity analyses, and positive controls designed to check whether the method preserves certain injected effects to be useful choices.

It is also appropriate that the manuscript explicitly defines KCOR as a model-dependent descriptive contrast, without directly assigning it a causal interpretation, particularly on **pages 4–7 and 34**. I therefore assess its coherence as a descriptive method first. Even at that level, several aspects need strengthening.

## 2. The main obstacle: diagnostics do not establish the origin of the curvature

The manuscript estimates $\theta$ by imposing, among other assumptions, a Gompertz baseline hazard:

$$
h_0(t)=k e^{\gamma t},
$$

with a fixed slope $\gamma$.

**Within this parametric family, $\theta$ can be identified.** A criticism asserting that frailty can never be estimated would therefore be excessive.

However, two questions must be distinguished:

1. Do the data allow a parameter to be estimated within the selected Gompertz–gamma model?
2. Do the data establish that this model actually describes the mechanism that generated the curve?

A good answer to the first question does not automatically provide a good answer to the second. The frailty-model literature describes precisely this difficulty: without strong restrictions, a changing baseline hazard and selection arising from unobserved frailty can produce the same observations. [3]

### An exact counterexample

Under the manuscript's model, the observed mortality hazard can be written as:

$$
h_{\mathrm{obs}}(t)
=
\frac{k e^{\gamma t}}
{1+\theta \frac{k}{\gamma}(e^{\gamma t}-1)}.
$$

This curve can be generated by a heterogeneous population in which the frailest individuals progressively die.

But a population **with no frailty heterogeneity**, in which every individual directly has this same time-varying mortality hazard, produces exactly the same curve. That variation could represent a medical, behavioral, or environmental change, or a time-dependent intervention effect.

In both cases, the survival data are identical.

Consequently, diagnostics based solely on those data can also be identical: the same residuals, the same goodness of fit, and the same parameter stability across windows.

**A perfect fit can therefore attribute the curvature to the wrong mechanism.**

The manuscript acknowledges this reasonably well on **page 12**, where it describes $\theta$ as an effective depletion-consistent parameter rather than a uniquely identified biological quantity. However, the assurances on **pages 14 and 38** that violations will be flagged or will lead to conservative behavior are too broad.

Diagnostics can detect some violations. They cannot guarantee that every competing explanation will be detected.

### Why this matters even for a descriptive contrast

KCOR can certainly be defined as the output of a mathematical transformation. That quantity then exists by definition.

But its scientific usefulness depends on the interpretation of that transformation: it is supposed to remove a relevant component of selection. If it instead removes a genuine change in risk that the analysis was intended to study, the result can be stable and correctly calculated while remaining misleading for the scientific question.

**Numerical stability and the validity of the interpretation therefore need to be assessed separately.**

## 3. I identified specific mathematical problems

These are not merely general reservations about observational studies. Some statements or equations in the PDF require correction.

### A. The claimed invariance of $\theta$ to multiplication of the observed hazard is incorrectly stated

On **page 11, Section 2.4.4**, the text explicitly considers multiplying the observed hazard by a constant:

$$
h_{\mathrm{obs}}^*(t)=c\,h_{\mathrm{obs}}(t),
$$

and states that this does not change the identification of $\theta$ when the multiplication is common across cohorts.

However, under the model as written, the equivalent representation is:

$$
\boxed{k^*=ck,\qquad \theta^*=\frac{\theta}{c}.}
$$

This follows directly:

$$
c\,\frac{\log(1+\theta H_0)}{\theta}
=
\frac{\log\left[1+(\theta/c)(cH_0)\right]}{\theta/c}.
$$

**The fitted frailty parameter therefore changes.**

What may remain invariant, when the same factor applies to both cohorts, is **the KCOR ratio**: both $H_0$ functions are multiplied by $c$, which cancels in the ratio.

This distinction is repairable, but important. The text needs to distinguish:

- the transformation of the parameters;
- the possible invariance of the final ratio;
- multiplication of the observed hazard;
- multiplication of the individual baseline hazard.

The last two operations are not equivalent in the presence of depletion.

### B. The discrete recurrence is not exactly the stated inversion

On **page 9**, the weekly quantity is defined as:

$$
h_j=-\log(1-d_j/N_j).
$$

This is an increment of the cumulative hazard.

On **page 12**, the reconstruction step uses:

$$
H_{j+1}
=
H_j+h_j(1+\theta H_j).
$$

But exact exponential inversion gives:

$$
\boxed{
H_{j+1}-H_j
=
(1+\theta H_j)
\frac{e^{\theta h_j}-1}{\theta}.
}
$$

The manuscript's recurrence is therefore a first-order approximation, valid when $\theta h_j$ is sufficiently small.

To assess the discrepancy, I performed an illustrative calculation with $\theta=30$, $h_j=0.001$, and 100 weeks:

| Calculation | Reconstructed cumulative hazard |
|---|---:|
| Recurrence written in the manuscript | 0.60729 |
| Exact exponential inversion | 0.63618 |
| Relative difference | −4.54% |

These numbers illustrate the equations; they are not results from the Czech data.

This does not establish that the published results are biased by 4.5%. **It shows that the time-step approximation and consistency between the two calculations need to be checked.**

### C. The linearity criterion must be consistent with Gompertz

**Page 14** requires an approximately linear cumulative hazard after normalization.

Yet under the chosen baseline:

$$
H_0(t)=\frac{k}{\gamma}(e^{\gamma t}-1),
$$

which remains curved as a function of $t$.

Linearity can be an approximation over a short window. It is exact as a function of the transformed time $(e^{\gamma t}-1)/\gamma$. The diagnostic should therefore specify what is being tested: **agreement with Gompertz, linearity in transformed time, or an approximation with a defined tolerance.**

## 4. The NPH component appears to be the least developed part

This is probably the section that most needs a rigorous derivation.

### The empirical result for $\alpha$ clearly fails to establish identification

On **pages 31–33**, the two methods produce minima around:

$$
\alpha \approx 1.18\text{–}1.19.
$$

However, the objective functions have little curvature, bootstrap estimates often accumulate at the boundaries of the search grid, and omitting certain cohorts substantially shifts the optimum.

The manuscript therefore concludes that **$\alpha$ is not reliably identified in this Czech application**.

That is a sound methodological decision. It nevertheless means that this version of the paper **does not demonstrate that it has corrected the NPH problem in this application**. Disabling an unidentified correction leaves the corresponding uncertainty unresolved.

### The factor defined in the text does not directly justify correction after inversion

On **pages 15–16**, the factor is defined as:

$$
F(t;\alpha)=E[z^\alpha\mid T>t].
$$

The manuscript then uses this factor to correct excess hazard **that has already been transformed by gamma-frailty inversion**.

To examine this change of scale, consider the schematic individual-level mechanism:

$$
h_i(t)=z_i b(t)+z_i^\alpha A(t).
$$

The observed cohort mortality hazard is then:

$$
h_{\mathrm{obs}}(t)
=
b(t)E[z\mid T>t]
+
A(t)E[z^\alpha\mid T>t].
$$

If linear selection is neutralized by dividing by the survivors' mean frailty, the remaining amplification factor for the wave component becomes:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{E[z^\alpha\mid T>t]}
{E[z\mid T>t]}.
}
$$

It is therefore necessary to explain why the post-inversion correction would use the moment alone rather than this ratio of moments.

The limiting case $\alpha=1$ makes the problem visible. The paper states that the module is then inactive. But its written factor is:

$$
F(t;1)=E[z\mid T>t].
$$

Under gamma-frailty depletion:

$$
E[z\mid T>t]
=
\frac{1}{1+\theta H_0(t)},
$$

which is generally less than 1. Dividing an already neutralized excess by this factor again changes it. The ratio of moments, by contrast, does equal 1 when $\alpha=1$.

**A normalization or justification is therefore missing from the equations as presented.** Special handling in the code might disable the module at $\alpha=1$, but the coherence of the formula near that value would still need to be demonstrated.

### A wave acting through $z^\alpha$ also changes the distribution of survivors

There is a deeper difficulty.

For the individual-level mechanism above, the frailty distribution among survivors is proportional to:

$$
f(z\mid T>t)
\propto
f_\Gamma(z)\,
e^{-zB(t)-z^\alpha W(t)},
$$

where $B(t)$ and $W(t)$ are the cumulative baseline and wave contributions.

When $\alpha\neq1$, this distribution **is generally no longer gamma**.

This means that the wave can change the shape of the frailty distribution, not merely its level. Gamma inversion applied to the whole trajectory then becomes an approximation; subsequent quiet windows can also be affected.

The manuscript presents its module as an assumption-dependent construction. That position is defensible, provided the approximation is shown to work sufficiently well. **I would request individual-level simulations in which deaths are actually generated by this mechanism**, followed by application of the entire KCOR procedure.

### Dividing a cumulative quantity by a time-varying factor requires additional justification

The correction on **page 16** divides cumulative excess by $F(t)$. But when this factor varies:

$$
\frac{C_{\mathrm{exc}}(t)}{F(t)}
\neq
\int_0^t \frac{dC_{\mathrm{exc}}(s)}{F(s)}
$$

in general.

The manuscript explicitly chooses a cumulative operator. That operator therefore needs its own derivation and validation. The instantaneous-hazard relationship is not sufficient to justify it.

## 5. The presented validation remains insufficient

The following points carry the most weight in my assessment:

| Manuscript component | What it establishes | What remains unresolved |
|---|---|---|
| Gamma simulations under the selected model, pp. 20–26 and Table 7 | KCOR can recover behavior close to the expected result when its assumptions match the data-generating mechanism. | Robustness when other mechanisms imitate the same curvature. |
| Positive controls, Table 9, p. 52 | The method preserves the direction of certain injected effects. | Effects present during estimation windows, prolonged effects, or frailty-dependent effects. |
| Empirical negative controls, Table 8, p. 51 | The procedure produces results relatively close to 1 in several comparisons. | Some deviations reach approximately 8–9%, with reported intervals excluding 1. |
| Interval coverage, Table 12, p. 55 | The validation procedure is described. | Every empirical coverage cell is still blank. |
| Comparison with shared-frailty Cox, Figure 5, p. 24 | An illustration of the expected attenuation is provided. | The curve is not obtained by actually refitting this model at each frailty level. |

### The 95% intervals have not yet been calibrated in this version

**Table 12** is explicitly presented as a table to be completed after a larger simulation. **Pages 18–19** acknowledge that definitive coverage conclusions are deferred.

This is transparent, but it means that **the proposed calibration has not yet been demonstrated in the PDF**. It does not establish that the intervals are wrong; it prevents their validity from being treated as established.

Crucially, because the method retains only analyses that pass its diagnostics, coverage needs to be measured **among accepted analyses**. The frequency with which an incorrect model nevertheless passes the diagnostics also needs to be measured.

Showing a few situations in which the system detects a problem is insufficient to establish that accepted analyses are reliable.

### Two negative controls need an explanation

**Table 8, page 51**, reports the following results, among others:

| Enrollment cohort | Comparison | KCOR | Reported interval |
|---|---|---:|---|
| 2022_06 | 2 versus 0 | 1.0756 | [1.055, 1.097] |
| 2022_06 | 2 versus 1 | 1.0911 | [1.070, 1.112] |

The table is presented as illustrating near-null behavior.

These results do not automatically refute the method: the pseudo-control could be imperfect, the target might require clarification, or residual bias could remain. But **deviations of 7.6% and 9.1% need to be explained**, particularly if differences of that magnitude are subsequently to be interpreted in an application.

### The positive controls are useful, but their scope is limited

Effects are notably injected during a period distinct from the windows used to learn frailty, on **pages 66–67**. This checks that KCOR does not systematically force every comparison to 1.

It does not, however, demonstrate that the method preserves an effect that:

- begins before or during the estimation window;
- evolves gradually in a way resembling the curvature attributed to frailty;
- changes frailty itself;
- depends on the level of frailty.

The results in Table 9 must also be evaluated against their actual cumulative target. An instantaneous multiplier of 1.2 applied during only part of follow-up does not necessarily imply a final KCOR of 1.2. I would therefore not treat the difference between them as an error without calculating the expected theoretical value.

## 6. Some formulations make the Cox comparison too favorable to KCOR

On **page 20**, the manuscript simulates one cohort without heterogeneity and another with gamma frailty, both without any intervention effect. It then states that the hazard ratio between cohorts is 1 by construction.

It is necessary to specify **which hazard ratio is meant**.

Under its own model:

$$
h_A(t)=h_0(t),
\qquad
h_B(t)=\frac{h_0(t)}{1+\theta H_0(t)}.
$$

The observed marginal ratio is therefore:

$$
\frac{h_B(t)}{h_A(t)}
=
\frac{1}{1+\theta H_0(t)},
$$

which generally does not equal 1.

The individual-level intervention effect is null, but the two populations genuinely have different marginal trajectories because of their composition.

A Cox model using only the cohort label can therefore produce a ratio different from 1 without having identified a causal effect. The proportional-hazards assumption is also violated in this construction.

**This simulation clearly demonstrates the danger of giving a causal interpretation to a difference between cohorts. It does not demonstrate that KCOR estimates the same quantity more accurately than Cox.** The manuscript itself acknowledges this difference in estimands in some passages.

The same qualification applies to restricted mean survival time (RMST): a difference in RMST between two different populations can correctly describe their observed survival. It does not automatically become bias because a frailty transformation subsequently produces a null contrast.

Existing survival methods can also produce adjusted survival curves and cumulative summaries; their scope extends beyond a single constant hazard ratio. They naturally retain their own identification assumptions. [2]

To assess KCOR's added value, **directly fitting a Gompertz–gamma model to the same aggregated data would be an important comparator**, using the same assumptions and a clearly defined target.

## 7. The final result requires a precise interpretation

### Correcting the variance does not remove every initial difference

Each cohort has its own scale parameter $k_d$. Even with perfect inversion, an initial difference in mean risk can therefore remain in the KCOR ratio.

For example, if:

$$
H_{0,A}(t)=m_A B(t),
\qquad
H_{0,B}(t)=m_B B(t),
$$

then:

$$
KCOR(t)=\frac{m_A}{m_B}.
$$

This ratio can differ from 1 without any intervention effect.

### Anchoring also removes a constant effect that is already present

The anchored version uses:

$$
KCOR(t;t_0)
=
\frac{KCOR(t)}{KCOR(t_0)}.
$$

If KCOR is constantly 1.3, its anchored version is constantly 1.

This removes initial level differences, whatever their origin. A constant multiplicative effect already present at the anchoring date can therefore be removed along with them.

**An anchored KCOR of 1.3 describes a relative change from the chosen anchor. It does not translate directly into “30% more deaths caused by the intervention.”** Moreover, the ratio concerns transformed cumulative hazards, not directly the observed proportions of people who died.

### An estimate close to zero does not, by itself, establish good identification

**Table S7, page 76**, gives practically zero estimates of $\theta$ for the Dose 2 cohorts, while **Table S6, page 75**, indicates that the diagnostics pass.

This is not necessarily contradictory: a value close to zero can be precisely estimated.

However, the text also describes such values as potentially indicating weak identification. It is therefore necessary to distinguish:

- an absence of depletion curvature estimated with precision;
- a boundary estimate arising because the data cannot resolve the question.

Objective-function profiles, intervals, and numerical criteria would be more informative than a sequence of “Yes” entries.

## 8. What I would request before trusting the method

As a reviewer, I would request **major revisions involving mathematical corrections and additional validation**:

1. **Correct the invariance statements and clarify the identification proposition.** The exact conditions need to be demonstrated, with a distinction between identification within the model and identification of the real mechanism.

2. **Fully derive the NPH module.** The individual-level mechanism, survivor distribution, post-inversion factor, and cumulative operator need to be connected, with coherent behavior at $\alpha=1$.

3. **Complete the validation of intervals and diagnostics.** This includes coverage after selection by the diagnostics and the frequency with which incorrect models are accepted.

4. **Test competing mechanisms capable of mimicking depletion.** Simulations should include changes in risk unrelated to frailty, effects overlapping the estimation windows, and waves that alter the survivor distribution.

5. **Explain the negative-control deviations and carry out the proposed comparisons.** Competing models should actually be fitted, with comparable or explicitly distinct targets.

6. **Bring the conclusions into alignment with the results.** Page 29 acknowledges that the direction of bias depends on the scenario; page 38 makes a much broader claim of conservativeness. The first position is better supported.

**I would therefore continue developing KCOR, but I would not regard this version as validated for an application whose conclusions depend heavily on the correction.** Gamma-frailty inversion provides a sound foundation. Attribution of the curvature, NPH correction, and statistical calibration remain the decisive issues.

My assessment concerns the supplied manuscript, its equations, and the results it presents. I checked the identities and illustrative algebraic examples discussed here, without rerunning the entire simulation suite or auditing its implementation.
