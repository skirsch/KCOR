# Independent review of KCOR candidate m177

**Review question.** Examine KCOR closely. What, if anything, is wrong that matters to the validity of the method or its results, and how would you fix it? Distinguish substantive flaws from minor corrections, and do not propose unnecessary redesigns.

**Verdict.** The proportional estimator has a well-defined descriptive target under its stated assumptions. I reproduced the seven Czech S6 point estimates and matched the current archived intervals and simulation summaries. I found two material implementation problems with a defined scope. One can silently compare different age populations in a partial ordinary workbook. The other makes a simulated replicate's pooled result depend on the other replicates in its workbook. Neither check implicated the published S6 estimates or Table 8.

## Material findings

1. **Moderate, partial-extract population reporting.** In `code/KCOR.py` around lines 6448–6473, when no full total is supplied, the direct aggregate sums each dose's available components independently. The label “All available ages” is assigned once per sheet. For a partial extract, an absent age-by-dose cell may be unreported even though people exist in it. The same label and an OK status conceal the difference in support. This is distinct from ordinary differences in age proportions between two completely observed populations.

   I used the candidate's 20-week, two-age, two-dose ordinary workbook fixture for enrollment 2023-01. I multiplied the late weekly deaths in the 1950 group by four for **both** doses after index 7 and rebuilt each opening risk history. With both groups supplied at both doses the anchored endpoint was 0.992923; within 1940 and 1950 it was 1.003326 and 1.006406. Removing only the 1950 dose-0 rows changed the reported aggregate to **2.217437**, still labeled “All available ages,” with normalization status OK. A three-draw workbook smoke test produced three valid draws and zero population-scope changes. The draws check software behavior only. The common observed age group's endpoint stayed 1.003326. The difference is reproducible without statistical model changes. `bootstrap_reporting.py` around lines 329–342 compares display labels across draws, which cannot detect a mismatch already present between doses in the point calculation.

   **Fix.** Record component support by dose before age bucketing. Where the partial extract cannot distinguish an omitted stratum from a documented zero enrollment, withhold the shared-population aggregate or label the two supports explicitly if the unequal-support comparison is intended. A common-support result can be computed and labeled separately. Keep valid individual age results. This applies to partial inputs and does not imply equal age proportions must be enforced in ordinary full cohorts.

2. **Moderate, batched Monte Carlo pooled results.** In `code/KCOR.py` around lines 4720–4735 the four-week weights are calculated over the sheet before the loop over `mc_id` and stored by sheet. The converter writes multiple iterations to the same sheet (`code/KCOR_CMR.py` around 1561–1579). This changes the pooled statistic in one independent simulated dataset as a function of other datasets.

   I ran the Monte Carlo workbook entry point with two age groups (1940, 1950), no direct aggregate, 20 weeks and dose 1 versus 0. The first replicate had a late mortality increase in just the 1940 dose-1 group. Run alone, its 1940/1950 weights were 0.409910/0.590090 and its final pooled KCOR was **1.588357**. I made no change to that replicate and batched it with a second, distinct replicate with 15 times as many people in the 1950 group. The weights became 0.079895/0.920105 and the first replicate's pooled endpoint became **1.096464**. This is a deterministic failure of equivalence between processing the same replicate alone and in a batch. Actual differences can be smaller with homogenous replicate sizes. If fixed external standardization weights are desired, they must be fixed by design independently of which replicates share a file.

   **Fix.** Calculate and carry each replicate's own weights through pooled estimation, status propagation and any pooled summaries. Test that both replicate order and splitting/combining sheets leave each individual result invariant. The ordinary person bootstrap refits one sampled enrollment at a time. I found no evidence that this Monte Carlo branch produced the paper's archived 999-draw Table 8 or S6 intervals.

## Scientific interpretation and independent numerical checks

- With known Gompertz slope and proportional gamma survivor history, the differential equation in Section 2.4.4 gives the stated parametric identification result. Without a baseline-shape restriction, any nonnegative candidate variance yields a corresponding baseline by applying the inverse to the same marginal cumulative hazard. The empirical data alone cannot identify the cause of the fitted curvature. The manuscript explicitly limits interpretation and defines the post-skip cohort-specific reference frailty. A causal effect or a universal direction of correction would require additional assumptions. Neither is needed to compute the stated descriptive target.
- I refitted the S6 post-skip, module-off analysis from `data/Czech/KCOR_CMR.xlsx`, sheet 2021_24 through 2024-16, using the current fitter and the supplied whole-population counts. The anchored all-ages result was **1.135551**. Ages 40–49 and 90–99 gave 1.507487 and 1.012162. All seven printed estimates agree at the reported precision. Substituting Nelson–Aalen increments throughout fitting and inversion changed each endpoint by less than 0.00012. Thus Section 2.3's sensitivity claim is substantively borne out for these points. Recomputing percentiles from `analysis/gap_assessment/s6/s6-all-window-draws.csv` gives [1.086022, 1.188725] for all ages and [0.960387, 2.821651] for ages 40–49.
- Slope sensitivity depends on the design. I refitted these same data with annual slopes 0.065 and 0.105, the perturbations illustrated in Section 2.4.3. The all-ages endpoints were 1.169085 and 1.127397. At ages 90–99 they were 1.041105 and 0.983779, compared with 1.012162 at 0.085. That group's ordinary interval already includes 1. The 0.4% movement reported in the paper is for its specific synthetic example. These Czech changes illustrate model dependence without overturning the descriptive results.
- From `analysis/finite_reference/table8-primary/identified_only-draws.npz`, excluding the original from percentiles, I verified the six printed proportional Table 8 points and intervals and all 999 residual-screen flags per comparison. For example, 2021-24 dose 2/0 gives 1.045599 [0.982249, 1.118734]. Intervals that include 1 with these flags do not validate the frailty law. The manuscript already reports the flags, uncertain chronology of window selection, and the absence of a known target in the empirical control. S6's separate retrospective product eligibility can select survivors using future treatment information. The manuscript quantifies exposure to that rule and acknowledges that its effect on KCOR is unknown. These issues would matter to causal interpretation, which this paper expressly disclaims.
- I recomputed S9's coverage, means, empirical variances and residual counts from the dataset-level rows in `analysis/misspecification/study/replicates.csv`. For example, gamma/person has mean 0.995226, variance 0.001426 and 98/100 coverage, while bimodal/person has mean 1.001715, variance 0.002053 and 92/100 coverage. These results support the tested null designs. The Cox comparison uses the known generating frailties. Table 9's injected benefit fails the signed gap diagnostic and remains an audit example. The optional Czech exponent is unidentified and is supplied for sensitivity. These are meaningful limits already reflected in the paper, not reasons to replace its estimator.
