**KCOR’s gamma-frailty inversion is mathematically sound. The main problem is the inference made from it.** If the frailty variance is known and individual hazards follow the stated multiplicative model, the inversion recovers the cumulative hazard for a person with unit frailty. The mortality curves alone cannot establish how much of their shape came from frailty. This edition also contains a false scale-invariance claim, a discrepancy in its main empirical figure, and unvalidated uncertainty intervals. I would recommend **major revision**. The defensible contribution is a descriptive sensitivity analysis showing how a cohort comparison changes under explicit assumptions about depletion. [Combined manuscript, pp. 7–14](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.pdf)

### 1. The manuscript’s own equations give a counterexample to its identification claim

Write \(G(t)=\int_0^t e^{\gamma s}\,ds\). In a quiet period governed by the model from entry, the observed hazard is
\[
h(t;k,\theta)=\frac{k e^{\gamma t}}{1+\theta kG(t)}.
\]
The paper explicitly claims that multiplying the **observed** hazard by a common constant \(c\) leaves curvature-based identification of \(\theta\) unchanged. But
\[
c\,h(t;k,\theta)=h(t;ck,\theta/c).
\]
Doubling every observed hazard therefore halves the fitted variance in an exact, noiseless fit. A common multiplier can cancel from the final *ratio* because both reconstructed cumulative hazards scale by \(c\). The individual variance estimates still change. Multiplying the **individual baseline hazard** before depletion is a different operation and can leave the underlying frailty variance fixed. The paper needs to specify which operation each claim and simulation uses. [Model and scale-invariance claim, §2.4.3–4](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:323)

The proposition also equates nonzero cumulative-hazard curvature with \(\theta>0\). Differentiating the model gives \(h'=\gamma h-\theta h^2\). Thus \(\theta=0\) already gives curvature when \(\gamma\ne0\), and \(\theta k=\gamma>0\) gives a constant observed hazard despite positive frailty variance. **The repair** is to state the narrower identification result. With known \(\gamma\), an exact positive quiet-period hazard obeys \(\theta=(\gamma h-h')/h^2\). The data must estimate the departure from the *specified baseline trend* precisely enough to be informative. Report a profile for \(\theta\) over plausible \(\gamma\) values, especially for boundary fits. Likewise, check the reconstructed hazard against the fitted Gompertz path. The expected cumulative hazard is \(kG(t)\), which is curved in ordinary time. [Identification proposition](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:345) · [Linearity diagnostic](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:454)

### 2. A perfect fit can remove a genuine time-varying difference

Consider two explanations for exactly the same cohort hazard. In the first, everyone has a Gompertz baseline and gamma-distributed frailty. In the second, everyone has identical frailty, but a time-varying exposure or background factor multiplies the Gompertz hazard by \(1/[1+\theta kG(t)]\). The two explanations produce identical survival curves, weekly death probabilities, quiet-window fits, and residuals. KCOR attributes the entire departure to depletion in both. More observations or more overlapping windows cannot distinguish them. Likewise, setting mean frailty to one separately in each cohort puts any difference in *mean* baseline vulnerability into \(k_d\). A difference in \(k_d\) survives inversion. [Individual-hazard model and inversion](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:275)

The discussion already acknowledges this ambiguity, but later says that violations lead to conservative behavior and that overlapping treatment effects show up as diagnostic failures. Those guarantees should be removed. A practical fix is to publish observed survival or cumulative risk alongside unanchored KCOR, show how the result changes under alternative baseline trends, and include the observationally identical example above in validation. Label post-skip results as conditional on surviving to the landmark. A four-week anchor also removes *any* constant ratio, including a genuine persistent difference. [Acknowledged ambiguity, §4.1](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:936) · [Conservativeness claims, §5.1–2](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:1023) · [Anchor definition](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:164)

### 3. The weekly estimator and Figure 1 need an end-to-end reconciliation

Step 2 uses an Euler update, while the final inversion uses an exact exponential. If \(q\) is the observed integrated hazard for one week and \(B_t\) is reconstructed cumulative hazard at its start, the exact update is
\[
B_{t+1}-B_t=(1+\theta B_t)\frac{\exp(\theta q)-1}{\theta}.
\]
Step 2 uses \((1+\theta B_t)q\). The discrepancy enters the wave offsets and hence the variance refit. There is a second issue when a wave precedes the first quiet window. An omitted positive cumulative offset \(C\) makes the seed hazard fit exactly as if its scale were \(k/(1+\theta C)\). Step 4 then freezes that scale. **The repair** is to use exact weekly increments throughout and estimate \(k\) and \(\theta\) jointly. A direct quiet-bin fitting equation is \(B_\theta(t+1)-B_\theta(t)=k\int_t^{t+1}e^{\gamma s}\,ds\), with \(B_\theta\) computed from the full observed history since the landmark. This also supplies an independent check on the delta iteration. [Steps 1–4](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:371) · [Final inversion](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:432)

Yet the Dose 2 endpoint falls from about **0.030** in panel A to **0.020** in panel B. The reference cohort’s endpoint also falls. The figure metadata identifies panel C as unanchored and gives its final ratio as 0.89487. A different accumulation origin or an additional transformation could explain the panels, but the caption does not identify one that accounts for the drop. Publish the weekly deaths, risk sets, accumulation origin, fitted parameters, and panel values for this example. ### 5. The stated 95% intervals have no demonstrated coverage

Resampling contiguous blocks of weeks can move an epidemic wave into a prespecified quiet period. Carrying the original risk sets with those blocks generally breaks the risk-set identity at block boundaries. Rebuilding the risk sets restores the arithmetic but pairs the resampled deaths with different denominators. Neither operation establishes the claimed preservation of counting-process covariance. All five empirical coverage cells in Table 12 are blank, including the correctly specified gamma null. [Bootstrap procedure](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:637) · [Table 12, PDF p. 55](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:1266)

For the stated fixed-cohort design with complete follow-up and independent individuals within strata, an aggregate bootstrap can resample the **counts by death week plus the count surviving to study end** as one multinomial vector per stratum. That keeps each death on its calendar week and yields coherent risk sets automatically. Refit the entire procedure, including data-estimated parameters and diagnostic gates. If shared shocks or clustering are part of the intended uncertainty, model that dependence explicitly. Report coverage against known generating truth, Monte Carlo error, the fraction of analyses retained, and coverage among retained analyses before describing the intervals as calibrated.

### 6. The optional wave correction does not follow from its hazard model

The paper defines \(F(t;\alpha)=E[Z^\alpha\mid\text{alive at }t]\) on the **observed excess-hazard** scale, then divides **accumulated, already-inverted** excess by the current \(F(t;\alpha)\). Dividing all prior excess by today’s factor generally differs from adjusting each increment at the time it occurred. There is a simple internal test. At \(\alpha=1\), the paper says the module is effectively inactive. Under ordinary gamma depletion, \(F(t;1)=1/(1+\theta B_t)\), which is below one after depletion. The published formula therefore changes a positive excess if applied at \(\alpha=1\). For a wave acting on individuals through \(Z^\alpha\) with general \(\alpha\), the survivor frailty distribution also need not remain gamma. [Wave model](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:508) · [Cumulative correction](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:568)

I would retain the paper’s decision to leave this module inactive for the Czech data. Before using it elsewhere, derive survivor selection and weekly excess increments from one individual-level model, verify exact identity at \(\alpha=1\), and test recovery of the *corrected cumulative trajectory* as well as recovery of \(\alpha\). An externally supplied intervention scale addresses one ambiguity in fitting the exponent. It does not validate the correction formula. [Czech identification result](/C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m17/documentation/preprint/paper.md:914)

**Revision priority:** reconcile Figure 1 and the weekly estimator first, then correct the identification proposition and validate parameter recovery and interval coverage. Those changes would let readers answer a worthwhile question: *How much does this observed cohort contrast change under a specified depletion model, and how sensitive is that answer to the model?* The current evidence cannot determine how much of the Czech mortality difference was caused by frailty depletion or by vaccination.
