### 1. A perfect fit can still remove a real mortality difference

Here is an exact example. Let \(B(t)=0.002(e^{\gamma t}-1)/\gamma\), with \(\gamma=0.085/52\). Suppose two cohorts have observed cumulative hazards \(H_A=\log(1+4B)/4\) and \(H_B=B\). A common Gompertz baseline with frailty variances 4 and 0 generates those curves exactly. KCOR then recovers identical baselines and an anchored ratio of **1**. The very same observations also arise in two homogeneous cohorts whose actual baseline cumulative hazards are \(H_A\) and \(H_B\). In that case their true week-100 baseline ratio, anchored at week 4, is **0.731**. Every weekly survival probability is identical across the two explanations. More people, smaller residuals, and shifting the quiet window cannot distinguish them. ### 5. Make the Czech wave and anchor assumptions visible in the primary result

The new wave solver has a defensible interpretation **conditional on the supplied exponent, wave dates, and baseline allocation**. There is one observed survival increment per week and two possible exposure increments, proportional \(b\) and wave \(w\). The solver supplies \(b\) from the fitted baseline. When observed mortality is too low, it assigns \(w=0\) and reduces \(b\). Exact reconstruction of survival therefore cannot verify how the deaths were allocated. Also, a common instantaneous wave amplitude cancels from a cross-cohort excess-hazard ratio conditional on survivor composition. Earlier wave exposure still changes that composition through \(e^{-ZB-Z^\alpha W}\). The abstract’s amplitude-invariance statement needs that qualification.
