### 3. Exact survival inversion still leaves wave attribution undetermined

During a wave, one weekly death count supplies one survival equation for two increments, baseline \(b\) and wave \(w\). KCOR closes that equation by keeping the planned baseline when observed mortality is high, and reducing the baseline while setting new wave exposure to zero when mortality is low. The paper now explains this convention. Its consequence deserves an empirical check: even in a correctly specified **zero-wave** population, upward count fluctuations can accumulate as positive wave exposure, while downward fluctuations cannot undo that exposure. A small numerical survival residual says the equation was solved. It says little about the recovered wave exposure. [Weekly allocation rule, §2.7.3](C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m94/documentation/preprint/paper.md:519) · [Implementation of the two branches](C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s4-approved/m94/code/kcor_survivor.py:79)

There is also a qualification to the abstract’s claim that common wave amplitude cancels. The *current* amplitude cancels in the cross-cohort excess-hazard ratio conditional on the survivor populations. Past amplitude helped determine those populations. For example, with \(\alpha=1\), common baseline exposure \(B\), common cumulative wave exposure \(W\), and unequal gamma variances, the ratio of survivor means is \((1+\theta_B(B+W))/(1+\theta_A(B+W))\). It still depends on \(W\). **Fix:** qualify the invariance claim, and add sampled zero-wave and misspecified-baseline experiments that report recovered \(W\), deficit-week frequency, and endpoint bias by cohort size. ### 5. The new validation supports a narrower, useful claim

In the main nonlinear simulation, the revised fit moved mean KCOR from **0.98982 to 1.00093** against a true value of one. Both methods covered in **90/100** datasets. In the stress design, the revised method covered in **48/50**, while its mean was **1.04019** and 47 of 50 original fits had a clamped gap. Those are genuine results: the specified survivor law improved average recovery in the main design, and appreciable bias remained possible in the stress design. Neither experiment establishes nominal 95% coverage throughout the intended applications. The new Fisher-information calculation is a useful precision benchmark under its assumptions, and the authors correctly distinguish it from calibration of the constrained estimator.
