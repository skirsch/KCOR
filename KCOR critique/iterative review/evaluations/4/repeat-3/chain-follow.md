## Addendum to the saved m94 review

**My recommendation remains major revision.** I would withdraw the earlier objections about fixed-rate fitting, approximate weekly integration, and the post-wave survivor calculation. m94 supplies both revised equations and a recovery experiment that bears directly on those objections. The most consequential remaining issue is what a successful fit establishes about mortality **outside** the fitting windows. This addendum leaves the saved open answer and the repository unchanged.

### Model and implementation

1. **Can KCOR create a contrast after fitting both quiet periods perfectly? Yes.** Consider an unmarked shock after the last quiet window. Let both cohorts have the same cumulative baseline exposure \(G\) and receive the same additional cumulative hazard \(W\), independent of individual frailty. For a cohort with gamma frailty variance \(\theta\), the observed cumulative hazard is \(H=\log(1+\theta G)/\theta+W\). Applying the paper’s ordinary inversion gives
   \[
   T_\theta(H)=e^{\theta W}G+\frac{e^{\theta W}-1}{\theta}.
   \]
   With \(G=0.05\), \(W=0.01\), and cohort variances 4 and 0.5, the resulting KCOR is **1.03264**, although both baseline and shock exposures are identical. Every quiet-window observation can follow the fitted model exactly. m94 now expressly acknowledges that counts cannot distinguish an unmarked nonlinear shock and that misspecification can pass the diagnostics. I would add this example beside the full-trajectory inversion and qualify the remaining claim that the distortion in an unadjusted comparison has a systematic direction. The objection to the older categorical claims about crossover and conservatism is resolved in §5.2. 2. **Are the curvature and hazard-scaling arguments wrong? The original errors are corrected. The slope-sensitivity claim remains unsupported.** The paper now derives \(h'=\gamma h-\theta h^2\). Thus a zero-frailty cohort can have a changing hazard, and a positive-frailty cohort can have a constant hazard when \(\theta k=\gamma\). It also distinguishes multiplying the observed cohort hazard by \(c\), which changes the fitted variance to \(\theta/c\), from multiplying each individual’s baseline hazard by \(c\), which preserves the variance. The revised quiet-window diagnostic uses linearity in Gompertz time. But §2.4 still claims that errors in the fixed slope \(\gamma\) cancel to first order and that a sensitivity analysis confirms this. At fixed observed \(h,h'\), its own equation gives \(\partial\theta/\partial\gamma=1/h\), so a common slope error can affect the two fitted variances differently. The supplement specifies a fixed slope and calls for a check. Its reported window scan varies dates. I would publish the actual slope grid with both fitted variances and the resulting KCOR, or withdraw the empirical confirmation. 3. **Does the fitter freeze the early baseline, approximate a week by its starting hazard, or omit exposure before the first quiet window? Those objections are resolved for the specified model.** Section 2.5 integrates each weekly increment, accounts for proportional exposure before first entry, and jointly optimizes \(k,\theta\) over the fitting weeks. The code reconstructs the offsets for each candidate parameter pair. The workflow figure still says that \(\hat k\) is held fixed and should be corrected. **Offset agreement remains a weak validation check.** The observed cumulative hazard at re-entry is used to construct the offset being checked. Moreover, the code clips each change relative to the *previous raw offset*. Successive raw offsets \(-a,0\) therefore produce applied offsets \(0,a\), even though the second raw offset is zero. In the stress experiment, 47 of 50 original datasets had a clamped gap. Show signed and applied offsets side by side and evaluate prediction in a held-out window. 4. **Does Figure 1 contradict the gamma inversion? No.** For the same nonnegative input, ordinary gamma inversion gives \(T_\theta(H)\ge H\). Figure 1 uses an earlier monthly slope correction. Its selection rule also restricted final KCOR to 0.85–1.15 and favored examples showing reduced curvature. That resolves the apparent arithmetic contradiction. The figure can illustrate the historical procedure, but a demonstration of the current estimator needs a current-method figure with a selection rule independent of the result. 5. **Are the in-wave and post-wave calculations still internally inconsistent? The specific survivor-calculation objection is resolved.** The revised method integrates the baseline from the first marked week, carries both proportional and wave exposure through the wave, and conditions subsequent survival on the survivor distribution at exit. The continuity test alone still takes exit exposure from the algorithm. Crucially, m94 also supplies an independent generating value. In a noiseless matched example, true exit exposure was 0.05 and the fitted value was 0.050000000009, with generating \(k\) and \(\theta\) recovered as well. That directly answers the old test objection. What remains assumption-dependent is the allocation of a week’s deaths between baseline and wave, especially when observed deaths fall below the fitted baseline. Recovery with known generating assumptions cannot verify that allocation in registry data. 6. **Does the Czech analysis establish \(\alpha=1.18\)? No, and m94 now says so explicitly.** The updated objectives reach the lower bound at 1.00, then reach the new lower bound when the grid extends to 0.70. Only 62 positive-excess cohort-weeks across eight cohorts enter that replay. I withdraw the objection that the manuscript presents 1.18 as an identified estimate. The reporting objection survives because Table 8 still gives the forced-1.18 sensitivity results without the primary wave-off estimates and intervals beside them. One further correction concerns the supplement’s interpretation of agreement between its two objectives. For the same cohort residuals \(r_i\) at a given time, \(\sum_{i<j}(r_i-r_j)^2=n\sum_i(r_i-\bar r)^2\). With equivalent weighting, agreement is algebraic. The paper should specify any weighting or data differences that make the comparison informative. ### Empirical interpretation and validation

7. **Do anchoring and the age-shift control validate removal of strong selection? Only the anchor-definition objection is resolved.** The paper specifies division by KCOR at one reference week. A persistent ratio \(K(t)=r\) consequently plots as one for *every* \(r\). The control tests subsequent drift and cannot establish recovery of the original level. The old 2022 estimates 1.0756 and 1.0911 are historical. All six current sensitivity intervals include one, and all 18,000 contributing fits succeeded without insufficient-signal flags. The authors correctly say that these pseudo-null contrasts have no known exact target and that inclusion of one does not establish equivalence. Yet §5.1 calls them a check under **strong** selection-induced curvature, while the supplement says this control has **reduced** depletion and expects small or weakly identified variances. I would revise §5.1 to match the actual control design. 8. **Do passing diagnostics distinguish negligible frailty from weak information? No.** The older window-scan outlier of 14.51 no longer applies. In m94 all 78 overlapping-window fits pass, although the 1940s dose-1 estimates span 0.00–1.56. Elsewhere, dose 2 has a fitted variance of \(7.01\times10^{-18}\) at ages 60–69. A boundary estimate can arise from genuinely small variance or from data that barely distinguish zero from materially larger values. The new information calculation explains why event volume and window length matter and expressly excludes a boundary-valid coverage guarantee. Report an upper uncertainty limit for \(\theta\) and propagate the supported range into KCOR. That would show whether weak frailty identification actually matters for a given endpoint. Yes.** The raw all-age ratio is 0.3159 while each displayed age-specific ratio exceeds one. The all-age row covers a wider age range, and the supplement labels its frailty fit as an independent pooled fit. 10. **Does the contamination study establish a threshold or false-positive rate? The earlier threshold criticism is substantially addressed.** m94 includes a zero-contamination comparison and distinguishes Monte Carlo uncertainty in the *mean* from the fraction of endpoints above one. At added weekly hazard 0.0003, the estimated mean shift is 0.010 with interval \([-0.012,0.032]\). At five million people per cohort, even 0.0001 produces a measurable shift. These results supersede a general “66% threshold.” The remaining attribution issue is concrete. Added deaths enter the fit, endpoint, and potentially the anchor. Moving the anchor alone changes the largest dynamic-contamination mean from 0.782 to 2.116. The paper acknowledges this. To quantify bias specifically from fitting a contaminated window, hold the evaluation data and anchor fixed and vary only the fit data. 11. **Are the Cox and RMST comparisons evidence of estimator bias? The target mismatch remains.** The simulation sets the treatment hazard ratio to one **at fixed frailty**. With homogeneous cohort A, heterogeneous cohort B, and common individual baseline exposure \(G(t)\), the marginal cohort hazard ratio is \(h_B/h_A=1/(1+\theta_B G(t))\). A cohort-only Cox fit therefore encounters a genuinely changing marginal ratio. Marginal restricted mean survival can likewise differ across these populations. Meanwhile, the illustrated KCOR inversion receives the generating variances, and the shared-frailty curve is approximated from a single null-case result. The manuscript discloses those choices but still calls the Cox result “spurious” and describes RMST as inheriting bias. Score each method against its own generating target, fit the shared-frailty model at each grid point, and distinguish known-parameter inversion from the fitted KCOR comparison. 12. **Are fitted recovery, positive controls, and person-level interval checks still missing? No. Their reach is limited in specific ways.** Table 9 now gives fitted week-80 positive controls with both cohorts refitted in 999 resamples. The injection multiplies the marginal weekly hazard among survivors during a finite window, so its multiplier is not itself the true week-80 cumulative ratio. Directional recovery is evidence of sensitivity to that injection. For Table 8, the bootstrap samples death-week and survivor categories, reconstructs risk sets, and restores overlapping group membership. I withdraw the objection that it samples weeks independently. For interval calibration, the current nonlinear experiment covers truth in **90/100** datasets and the stress experiment in **48/50**. The latter also has mean KCOR **1.04019** against a null of one, with 241 insufficient-signal bootstrap draws despite zero flagged originals. Report coverage, bias, interval width, and flag frequency together by design. A successful original fit alone does not settle precision. The revision gives credible evidence that KCOR recovers the intended quantities when data follow its specified survivor law. The publication claim I would narrow is that stable fits and reassuring controls establish depletion-neutralized mortality in the registry comparison. The common-shock example shows exactly where that inference can fail. The most informative additions would be the primary wave-off Table 8 with intervals, an actual \(\gamma\)-sensitivity analysis, and held-out predictions across quiet-window boundaries.
