### 1. A zero wave can erase a correctly estimated frailty variance

I generated 100 exact weekly hazards from the paper’s model with \(k=0.001\), \(\gamma=0.085/52\), \(\theta=4\), and no wave or sampling noise. The current joint fitter returned \(\hat\theta=3.9999999998\) with weeks 0–99 designated quiet. With weeks 0–39 and 60–99 designated quiet, the seed still recovered \(4.0000000000\). At re-entry the calculated excess was \(-4.16\times10^{-17}\), numerically zero. The fitter then returned **\(\hat\theta=0\), `success=True`, `status="insufficient_signal"`**. The exact weekly reconstruction is correct. The decision made from its output is wrong. A gap with zero excess is entirely compatible with positive, well-identified frailty. [Weekly hazard generator](C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s3-8d29/m83/code/KCOR.py:1126) · [Offset calculation and zero-frailty return](C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s3-8d29/m83/code/KCOR.py:1469)

**Fix.** Allow zero wave exposure while retaining the fitted frailty. Use a numerical tolerance for roundoff and quantify the sampling uncertainty of materially negative offsets. A negative estimated excess can challenge the wave model without establishing anything about whether \(\theta=0\). Add this noiseless case as a regression test, then test the frequency and consequences of the fallback on integer death counts.

### 5. The Czech contamination bound and the interpretation of anchoring are unsupported

The paper still identifies a sign-reversal threshold at contamination equal to 66% of baseline mortality and uses it to defend the Czech analysis. In the comorbidity-only simulation, **zero contamination** already gives mean anchored KCOR **1.034**, with **15/20** runs above one. At 66%, the mean is **1.062**, with **13/20** above one. The underlying identification limit has a simple expression. For an observed cumulative hazard \(H\), every candidate \(\theta\geq0\) gives the same observed survival if one chooses \(B_\theta=\operatorname{expm1}(\theta H)/\theta\), with \(B_0=H\). The fixed Gompertz slope supplies the restriction that separates these explanations. Even within that model, the paper’s own formula implies that a common slope error \(\Delta\gamma\) changes the locally inferred variance by \(\Delta\gamma/h_d\), which differs by cohort. Likewise, if the normalized ratio is a constant \(c\), anchoring gives \(c/c=1\), including when \(c\) represents a genuine sustained effect. Report observed absolute risks, the unanchored normalized ratio, and the anchored change side by side. Identify the population as survivors at the end of the skip, grouped by exposure at enrollment.
