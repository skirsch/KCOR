# Scientific review of the current KCOR edition

**Major revision.** KCOR has a defensible use as a descriptive sensitivity analysis. Given a specified unit-mean gamma frailty variance, the inversion from marginal survival to cumulative hazard at frailty one is correct. The revision also corrects the hazard-scaling law, derives identification with known Gompertz slope, uses exact week-boundary increments in the core fit, and discloses that the Cox comparison used the generating frailty variances. I would retain those changes. The main obstacle is that the published estimator, the default registry estimator, and the rule for reporting an estimate still differ in consequential ways.

**1. The Czech results violate the paper’s reporting rules.** The paper says that an unidentified wave exponent is inactive, and concludes that Czech α is unidentified. Yet the revised Table 8 calculation requires α = 1.18. There is a separate problem even with α fixed. Four component fits in the original 2022 analysis had insufficient signal. All 999 bootstrap draws for that enrollment and 307 of 999 for 2021 had at least one such flag. The code computes the pooled ratios anyway and takes quantiles over all draws. For example, the 2022 ratio of 1.046 has an interval of 0.322–1.268. Inclusion of one across all six intervals gives little reassurance about accuracy, especially as the age-shift control has no known horizontal mortality trajectory. Recompute primary results with the wave module inactive, reserve forced α for labeled sensitivity analyses, and publish a component-by-component account of fitted values, fallback use, and interval width. Specify what population quantity a fallback estimates and what happens to a pooled contrast if a component is unidentified. Simply discarding failed bootstrap draws would introduce its own selection problem. Locations: paper lines 94–98, 485, 619, 671, 818–820, 927, 1237–1246; SI lines 58–70; `analysis/table8/table8_bootstrap.py` lines 43–51, 80–98, 162–172; `analysis/table8/table8_bootstrap.json`, both enrollment summaries.

**2. Make the normal analysis path implement the published equations.** Section 2.5 jointly estimates k and θ in the first quiet window, with a final refit on the union of quiet windows. Joint estimation exists, but defaults to false. The ordinary age-specific and all-age workbook calls omit the switch. Table 8 explicitly enables it. Also, the final fitting mask includes the early anchor weeks even when outside all quiet windows, and even joint mode first requires a usable early hazard average. Thus two datasets identical throughout the specified fitting windows can produce different fits or eligibility solely because they differ during a purportedly excluded early interval. This matters given the paper’s motivation for early stabilization. In the 2022 Table 8 data, replacing the joint fit by the early-fixed fit with everything else held fixed changes 1 versus 0 from 1.0462 to 1.0073 and 2 versus 0 from 1.0677 to 1.0129. Implement one explicit estimator in the normal entry point, publish all fit bins and boundary/fallback rules, and regenerate the results through that entry point. Include a regression example with a quiet window well after the anchor. Locations: paper lines 371–394, 422–436, 820; SI line 306; `code/KCOR.py` lines 1133–1143, 1233–1255, 1373–1395, 1544–1555, 7006–7015 and 7315 onward; `analysis/table8/compare.py` lines 8–10.

**3. Separate estimability under a specified baseline from evidence that the baseline is appropriate.** The new identity θ = (γ − d log h/dt)/h is correct for the exact model. For any observed cumulative hazard H and any candidate θ, however, the alternative baseline B = expm1(θH)/θ reproduces precisely the same survival, including B = H at θ = 0. An analyst observing these deaths cannot tell whether a declining mortality rate came from depletion or from a falling underlying hazard. A shared slope error Δγ changes locally inferred θ by Δγ/h, which can differ greatly between cohorts. First-order cancellation in the final ratio needs evidence for the actual follow-up and strata. The revised Czech scan is particularly informative: all 78 fits pass, yet dose-1 θ ranges from effectively zero to 7.70 in one birth decade and to 14.51 in the other. Passing is defined there by solver success, an “ok” status and absence of a degeneracy flag. The 13 overlapping windows are not independent validation datasets. Show the range of the **final ratios** across prespecified baseline slopes, windows and plausible alternative baseline histories. Publish numeric rules for stability and predictions in held-out periods. If estimates with substantively different interpretations pass, report the range rather than certifying the result. Keep the paper’s new acknowledgment that stable bias can escape these checks. Locations: paper lines 332–359, 464–481, 1043–1056; SI lines 302–310, 366–378, 398–421; `analysis/quiet_windows/scan.py` lines 62–80.

**4. Person-level resampling fixes a real defect, but interval calibration remains estimator-specific.** With complete fixed-cohort follow-up, sampling disjoint person/event-week cells and rebuilding the risk sets is an appropriate way to reproduce resampling of complete records. The historical expected-count/Poisson pilot cannot assess coverage, as the revision now explains. The person-bootstrap results are more informative. In the separate million-person study, the early-fixed fit gave 502/600 coverage with all 600 datasets passing. The paired results file also reports *on the same 200 datasets* 160/200 covered with the early-fixed fit and 194/200 with the joint fit. The joint intervals were about three times wider on average (0.149 versus 0.048), and the joint point estimator had larger RMSE (0.0375 versus 0.0136). Include the paired result alongside the separate studies, identify its precise code revision and generating process, and avoid interpreting improved coverage as improved point accuracy. The paper acknowledges its two cited studies predate the final week-boundary equations. Provide a runnable calibration for the shipped version with analytic population truth, multiple quiet windows, waves, sparse cells and boundary cases. Report coverage, error, width and acceptance rates by scenario. The sparse scenario, for example, has zero accepted datasets, so conditional coverage there cannot be estimated. Locations: paper lines 642–671; `analysis/table8/table8_bootstrap.py` lines 24–40 and 63–72; `validation/person_bootstrap/design.json`, `summary.json` and `paired.json` (summary at top). The latter records a source hash, which differs from the supplied `code/KCOR.py` bytes.

**5. The wave-exit equation is useful, but it relies on an unresolved in-wave attribution.** Conditional on known baseline and wave exposures at exit, the ratio of the tilted Laplace transforms in §2.7.3 correctly gives subsequent conditional survival. The revised algorithm also includes the first wave week. During the wave it still computes a mean-one gamma moment at the updated scale. Under baseline-only depletion the survivor distribution retains its original shape 1/θ and has updated scale s = θ/(1+θB). At θ = B = 1 the second survivor moment is 0.5; treating s = 0.5 as the variance of a fresh mean-one gamma gives 1.5. During a nonlinear wave the survivor distribution is generally no longer gamma. Dividing accumulated excess by today’s factor also rescales earlier weeks retroactively. The paper now calls this an approximation; its quantitative error matters when α is forced in Table 8. Moreover the no-wave increment there is k exp(γt), while the revised core uses that quantity times expm1(γ)/γ. Use the same integrated increment throughout. With a specified baseline path B(t) and α, directly solve E[exp(−ZB(t)−Z^αW(t))] = S(t) for cumulative wave exposure, assess monotonicity and infeasible weeks, and validate recovered exposures and the final ratio on independently generated individual histories. This makes the external assumptions explicit. Locations: paper lines 508–568, 575–607; `code/KCOR.py` lines 1028–1047, 1241–1247, 2491–2541, 2658–2677, 2699–2722; `test/test_nph_post_window_continuity.py` lines 25–59.

**6. Withdraw the claimed 66% contamination threshold.** In the source table the comorbidity-only series already has mean anchored KCOR 1.034 at *zero* contamination, with 15/20 replicates above one. At the alleged threshold the mean is 1.062, with 13/20 above one. The intermediate mean is 0.989. Thus the statements about monotonic increase and first crossing at 66% do not hold over the published grid including its control. An intervention effect equal to zero is also not the same as a null all-cause contrast after adding actual extra deaths to only one group. The injection begins in the anchor week, so extra deaths, movement of the anchor and frailty-estimation error all contribute. Define the population target under each perturbation, compare the fitted result with oracle and raw estimates, use paired stochastic draws and enough repetitions to resolve the change from zero, and report uncertainty for a prespecified meaningful error. Repeat with the selected final estimator. There is no basis here for bounding unmeasured Czech confounding by a single percentage. Locations: paper lines 849–879; `test/quiet_window_contamination/out/quiet_contamination_agg.csv` rows 2 and 7–9; `test/quiet_window_contamination/run_contamination_test.py` lines 151–175, 195–215.

**7. Compare like scientific targets.** With two cohorts having the same individual baseline and mean-one frailty but variances 0 and θ, the marginal hazard ratio for the heterogeneous cohort is 1/(1+θB(t)). It genuinely departs from one, and marginal RMSTs genuinely differ. The Cox experiment now correctly discloses oracle θ and supplies person-bootstrap intervals conditional on those known values. It demonstrates the algebra of inversion under the generator; the one-run curve and an approximate shared-frailty reference cannot establish comparative bias of fitted methods. Show oracle and estimated KCOR side by side against their population targets over repeated datasets; benchmark marginal methods against marginal truths. For real cohorts present observed absolute risks and standardized differences next to unanchored and anchored KCOR. Anchoring a permanent true proportional difference or a permanent selection difference removes either one. A post-skip contrast also excludes early deaths and conditions on surviving the skip. These distinctions determine what question each display answers. Locations: paper §§2.1.1, 2.2, 2.7, lines 698–745 and 1257–1272.

The strongest next result would be a reproducible analysis of the *prespecified final* KCOR contrast, including how often a meaningfully wrong estimate passes the full reporting rule under realistic alternative trajectories. The existing revisions make that question easier to ask. They do not yet demonstrate that registry mortality determines a unique depletion adjustment, or that the reported primary Czech intervals measure its uncertainty reliably. I checked the equations, current source, supplied changes and committed numerical outputs without rerunning national registry processing or the full Monte Carlo studies.
