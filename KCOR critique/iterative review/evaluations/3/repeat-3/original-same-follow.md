# Targeted follow-up on objections whose status was implicit

1. **Common hazard scaling and curvature. The original mathematical objections are resolved.** For a common multiplier c of the *observed* hazards, the fitted model parameters become (ck, θ/c). The inverted cumulative hazards then each acquire the factor c, so their ratio is invariant even though the variances change. For a multiplier of the *individual baseline*, θ stays fixed. The current §2.4.4 makes the distinction correctly. It also gives h′=γh−θh², explains the curved θ=0 and flat positive-θ cases, and confines its identification proposition to known γ and the model. The separate claim about first-order cancellation of *slope misspecification* in §2.4.3 is still unsupported as a general conclusion. This is a change of γ, not a common multiplicative transformation of observed hazards. [Current paper, lines 321–361.]

2. **Linearity, diagnostic sufficiency and θ near zero. The primary diagnostic has been corrected.** Section 2.6.2 now compares the inverted trajectory to the fitted cumulative Gompertz curve plus its offset; linearity holds on the transformed Gompertz time axis. The paper also now explicitly acknowledges that model-compatible disturbances can pass all checks, as does the SI. Neither objection should be repeated as though the revision had ignored it. Two narrower inconsistencies remain. The algorithm summary still lists “post-normalization linearity”, and §2.6.2 still describes a near-zero estimate as indicating weak identification. A precise estimate at the zero boundary and an imprecisely estimated variance require different interpretations. Report a profile or interval and say which scale is used for the shape check. [Paper, lines 463–481, 1220; supplement, lines 24–28, 359–365, 410–420.]

3. **Mean frailty, the landmark and the orientation of comparisons. These qualifications still apply.** Defining a separate mean-one frailty for each cohort folds any initial mean-risk difference into its baseline k. Inversion can leave an arbitrary difference in k even if there is no intervention effect. Conversely, anchoring divides out any constant ratio of inverted baseline hazards, including a genuine constant effect. This is exact and needs no pathological data. The paper does distinguish the two estimands and now explicitly acknowledges missed acute effects during the skip. But the risk-set and skip equations imply that post-skip survival starts among people who survived to that landmark, whereas the SI raw hazards are accumulated from enrollment. Its Dose 0/Dose 2 ratio is 0.3159 overall, versus 1.2776–2.1343 in every displayed age band; the overall row also includes younger ages. Neither these differently composed populations nor raw and adjusted estimates with different origins supply a like-for-like assessment of normalization. Use the same origin, orientation, age population and weights for paired displays; give early risks separately. [Paper §§2.1.1, 2.3, 2.4.1, 2.7, 2.8, 5.2; supplement §S6.1.2, especially lines 412–437.]

4. **Early-anchor bias and treatment of sparse fits. These persist on specific code paths.** The revised joint-seed path fits both parameters. The default fixed-k path still averages observed early weekly hazards divided by unit-baseline increments. With positive θ, a frailty-depleted observed increment is smaller than its individual baseline increment even without noise. That bias objection concerns the fixed-k path; it should not be attributed to the joint optimizer. Both paths still request early anchor data and include them in the final fit. Both can return θ=0 and success for insufficient deaths; false certainty from treating that as homogeneity remains a reporting problem. The Euler reconstruction objection is resolved for both paths. [`code/KCOR.py:1133–1155,1234–1290,1375–1393,1461–1467,1549–1559`; paper §2.5.]

5. **The Cox bands, monthly example and RMST comparison have different statuses.** The missing-oracle-disclosure and missing-error-bar objections no longer apply to the revised Cox figure. The caption accurately identifies person-bootstrap error bars for a known-θ inversion; those bars cannot validate fitted θ. The monthly slope-removal example now discloses the earlier algorithm and outcome-dependent selection, so the provenance criticism is resolved while the limitation as evidence for the revised method remains. The separate RMST objection still applies: observed groups can have genuinely different restricted mean survival under identical conditional treatment hazards. The paper explains this estimand difference in §2.1, yet the table caption still calls it “depletion bias”. It also now explicitly identifies the grid's earlier single-window fitter. [Paper, lines 119–121, 136–138, 709–745, 1266–1272; `analysis/cox_intervals/figure_intervals.py:18–61`.]

6. **The old pilot's numerical results are historical, and the time-block objection is resolved for the new bootstrap.** The archived gamma-null 35.4%/18.8% θ coverages and four 100% KCOR coverages still exist. They describe the old single-window estimator, deterministic outer datasets in four scenarios, and a fitted reference-draw target. They are not measurements of the current estimator's coverage. Section 2.9.1 now explicitly makes that limitation clear and specifies sampling complete people. Table 8 preserves shared memberships using disjoint event/survivor categories. Thus the original objection to breaking risk-set continuity by swapping blocks does not apply to that table. General calibration of release outputs remains unsettled: the identified historical and paired studies have differing configurations; Table 8 retains flagged fits; and a workbook path still computes analytic post-anchor intervals. [Paper, lines 642–671, 820; `test/sim_grid/code/generate_sim_grid.py:388,866`; `test/sim_grid/code/compute_bootstrap_coverage.py:201–226`; `test/sim_grid/out/bootstrap_coverage*.csv`; `analysis/table8/table8_bootstrap.py:24–40,65–98`; `code/KCOR.py:4451–4480`.]

7. **The quiet-window threshold objection is unchanged, including its dependence on estimator version.** The reported zero-addition comorbidity mean already exceeds one, and sampling error is appreciable at 20 replicates per cell. The harness uses skip+4 as its anchor and calls the fitter without enabling its joint-seed option. The reported numbers cannot be carried over as a threshold for the revised joint estimator or as a bound on Czech confounding. The direct effect of adding to the outcome hazard must also be separated from the indirect change in fitted θ. [Paper §3.3.2; `test/quiet_window_contamination/out/quiet_contamination_agg.csv:2,7–10`; `test/quiet_window_contamination/run_contamination_test.py:150–175,195–215,299–305`; `code/KCOR.py:815,1142`.]

8. **Wave survivor moments, α=1 and the post-wave tail. The earlier objection applies during a nonlinear wave, not to the revised conditional tail equation.** Under ordinary proportional gamma depletion, survivors retain gamma shape 1/θ, with mean 1/(1+θB), variance θ/(1+θB)² and scale θ/(1+θB). The helper returns the scale and the moment routine evaluates a fresh mean-one gamma. For α≠1 its output is a proxy for the survivor moment. The paper and SI now expressly acknowledge this, but the localization equations still motivate F using the true survivor moment, and α fixed from elsewhere cannot repair a wrong factor. For α=1 the *correction* is rightly an identity because ordinary gamma inversion already recovers the combined proportional hazard; this does not require the actual surviving mean frailty to equal one. The old constant post-wave offset has been replaced by a conditional joint-survival inversion. That is correct given B and W at exit. The remaining accuracy problem is obtaining these exposures from accumulated excess, division by its current factor and a monotonicity repair. The new regression test conditions on the fitted W. [Paper §§2.7.1–2.7.3; supplement §S2.1; `code/KCOR.py:1028–1047,2491–2541,2658–2726`; `test/test_nph_post_window_continuity.py:32–56`.]
