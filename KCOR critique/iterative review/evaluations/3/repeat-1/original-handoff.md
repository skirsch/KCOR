**I recommend major revision.** KCOR’s gamma-frailty inversion is mathematically sound under its stated model, and this edition corrects two important problems from the previous review. It gives the right identification result for a fixed Gompertz slope and uses the exact week-boundary inversion. The main remaining problem is empirical. The fitter can replace a correctly identified positive frailty variance with zero, those replacements enter reported intervals, and a prominent positive control reproduces without running KCOR at all. I would retain KCOR as a model-based descriptive sensitivity analysis and narrow the validation claims. ### 1. The fitter can erase real frailty on perfect model data

I tested the current joint-fit function in memory with 100 noiseless weekly hazards generated by its own equation, \(k=0.001\), \(\gamma=0.085/52\), true \(\theta=4\), and no wave. With quiet weeks 0–29, it returned \(\hat\theta=4\) to numerical precision. Adding an equally valid quiet window at weeks 60–89 made it return **zero**, with `success=True` and `status="insufficient_signal"`. The initial estimate was still \(3.99999999999997\). The reconstructed gap offset was \(-5.55\times10^{-17}\). Its population value is exactly zero. The code interprets *any* nonpositive first-pass offset as a reason to discard the variance estimate. A gap with no excess deaths is compatible with strong, well-identified frailty. [Weekly model and fitter](<C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s3-8d29/m83/code/KCOR.py:1126>) · [Offset decision and zero return](<C:/Users/jasmi/AppData/Local/Temp/kcor-s3-8d29/m83/code/KCOR.py:1474>)

The ordinary workbook also uses a different estimator from the main methods section. It fixes \(k\) from the first post-skip hazards. Table 8 explicitly enables joint estimation of \(k,\theta\) in the first quiet window. Both paths add early anchor weeks to the final objective, including when those weeks fall outside the stated quiet windows. **Fix:** use one named estimator through the workbook, simulations, and paper. Fit weekly deaths against their risk sets with \(D_t\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(Y_t,1-e^{-q_t})\), using the published exact weekly increment \(q_t\). Let a gap have zero exposure, estimate its offset separately from frailty, and distinguish an estimated boundary value from an unsupported fit. Keep the noiseless two-window example as a regression test. ### 3. The positive control and Cox benchmark test less than the paper claims

**Table 9’s positive-control results reproduce from raw hazards alone.** From the supplied positive-control workbook, I summed the observed weekly hazards, divided the treated cumulative hazard by the control cumulative hazard, and anchored that ratio at index 4. At week 80 I obtained **1.106984 for harm and 0.824596 for benefit**, exactly the published 1.107 and 0.825 after rounding. This is the plotting script’s raw-data calculation. It estimates no frailty parameter and performs no inversion. The generator multiplies the *marginal hazard among survivors* and uses expected deaths. These results demonstrate that the injected signal is visible in the input. Section 3.2 credits them to the revised estimation pipeline. **Fix:** retain the raw curve as a comparator, inject an effect into individual hazards, derive the resulting population cumulative target, then run the released fitter on independent sampled cohorts. ### 5. The wave correction has a sound conditional tail and an unresolved wave exposure

The new post-wave equation is correct **given** baseline exposure \(B_e\) and wave exposure \(W_e\) at wave exit. The test independently integrates survival after taking \(W_e\) from the correction routine itself. It therefore checks the post-wave inverse. Inside the wave, the code still divides accumulated excess by a time-varying mean-one gamma moment. Even before nonlinear wave selection, with starting variance one and accumulated baseline one, survivor frailty is exponential with mean \(1/2\). Its second moment is \(1/2\), while the implemented proxy gives \(1.5\). A wave proportional to \(z^\alpha\) further changes the survivor distribution. There is also a smaller discretization mismatch: the wave reference adds \(ke^{\gamma t}\) per week, while the revised fitter integrates the Gompertz hazard across that week. ### 6. Passing the Czech checks does not establish removal of selection

For observed cumulative hazard \(H\), every \(a\geq0\) admits the baseline \(B_a=\operatorname{expm1}(aH)/a\), with \(B_0=H\). The Gompertz restriction supplies the identifying information. A good fit can reveal compatibility with that restriction, but cannot establish that a smooth hazard change arose from frailty. The revised window scan illustrates the distinction particularly well. **All 78 fits passed**, while dose-1 variance ranged from effectively zero to **7.70** in one birth decade and **14.51** in the other. The Czech configuration also says 2023 was chosen because it showed the least vaccine harm. That makes a dated, outcome-independent window specification important to the interpretation.
