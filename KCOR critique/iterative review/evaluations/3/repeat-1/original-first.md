# Independent review of KCOR

**I would retain the gamma-frailty inversion as a model-based sensitivity analysis and revise the manuscript before relying on its empirical validation.** For a fixed cohort with mean-one gamma frailty multiplying a common time path, the Laplace-transform identity and its inverse are correct. A prespecified landmark and a descriptive comparison of those reconstructed paths are reasonable. The main problems are what the data identify, which estimator the experiments actually test, and what uncertainty readers can attach to a reported contrast.

1. **The identification proposition confuses observable curvature with frailty.** Write the marginal hazard as $q$. Under the stated quiet-period Gompertz–gamma model, direct differentiation gives $q'=q(\gamma-\theta q)$. With a known slope and positive hazard, the population value is $\theta=(\gamma-q'/q)/q$. If $\theta=0$, the cumulative hazard still curves whenever the Gompertz slope is positive. Conversely, $\theta=\gamma/k>0$ gives a constant marginal hazard and an exactly straight cumulative hazard. With an unrestricted baseline, *every* candidate variance $a\ge0$ fits the same observed curve by assigning baseline cumulative hazard $(\exp(aH)-1)/a$, using its continuous limit at zero. A constant multiplier $c$ on the **observed** hazard changes the fitted variance to $\theta/c$ and the baseline scale to $ck$. This differs from multiplying the individual baseline before depletion. A common multiplier can cancel from a ratio while changing both fitted variances. Replace the proposition and invariance claim with the correct identities, profile uncertainty in the slope and variance together, and test the fitted Gompertz increments in held-out windows. Quiet-period fit alone cannot distinguish depletion from a smooth change in baseline risk. See paper lines 273–319, 321–359 and 454–474.

2. **The implemented fit differs materially from the published algorithm.** Section 2.5 jointly estimates scale and variance in the first quiet window, then holds scale fixed. The production code fixes scale to the mean of the first post-skip hazards divided by the Gompertz growth factor, even outside quiet weeks. It fits only variance in the first quiet segment and includes anchor weeks in the pooled objective. Even under the stated model, the anchor mean contains the depletion factor $1/(1+\theta H_0)$. The reconstruction also uses an Euler update on weekly *integrated* hazards. The exact increment from a weekly observed integrated hazard $u$ is $(1+\theta H_0)\operatorname{expm1}(\theta u)/\theta$. It can differ materially during a wave. Implement one grouped-binomial likelihood using exact weekly survival probabilities and the same inversion for fitting and reporting. Preserve signed gap offsets if allowed by the scientific model and flag unsupported fits explicitly. See paper lines 371–428 and code/KCOR.py lines 1226–1237, 1321–1377, 1392–1394, 1449–1501 and 1534–1555.

3. **The headline Cox benchmark gives KCOR the answer to its hardest estimation problem.** The text says both methods receive identical information and KCOR estimates each cohort’s variance. The generator passes the true simulated variance directly into the inversion. The saved final row reproduces the paper’s Cox HR of 0.551 and KCOR of 1.024. Moreover, when one arm has variance zero and both have the same individual baseline hazard, the *marginal* hazard ratio B/A is $1/(1+\theta_B H_0(t))$. It genuinely differs from one. A marginal survival or restricted mean survival difference also exists in that population. Calling all such differences bias conflates scientific targets. Keep the known-variance result as an algebra check. Add repeated trials with production-estimated variance, identical observed inputs, and a separately calculated population target for each method. See paper lines 687–728, 1216–1226 and 1257–1263, test/sim_grid/code/generate_cox_bias_sim.py lines 209–235 and 278–285, and test/sim_grid/out/cox_bias_results.csv.

4. **The coverage experiment cannot calibrate the advertised intervals.** The methods resample contiguous time blocks. The coverage code draws independent weekly Poisson counts around the observed deaths, truncates to the remaining risk set, and defines truth by rerunning its own estimator on one reference dataset. Its gamma scenarios generate deterministic fractional expected deaths, including the “sparse” case. The fitted model uses a single-window constant-baseline cumulative-hazard fit. In the committed 500-dataset, 200-bootstrap run, four scenarios show 100% coverage and the non-gamma scenario shows 25.4%. The gamma-null target is 1.00944 even though the common latent baseline implies a ratio of 1. More repetitions will reproduce these design problems. Draw independent subjects or sequential binomial deaths for each outer dataset, use the known latent cumulative hazards for truth, and run the released estimator and its actual interval routine. For fixed cohorts with complete follow-up, one aggregate-data bootstrap samples the vector of death-week counts and survivors multinomially within each stratum, preserving the original calendar and risk-set history. Report diagnostic acceptance, unconditional and conditional coverage, and Monte Carlo uncertainty. Trace each published band to its routine because the standard output also contains log-scale Wald intervals. See paper lines 637–662 and 1266–1274, test/sim_grid/code/compute_bootstrap_coverage.py lines 85–125 and 201–225, test/sim_grid/code/generate_sim_grid.py lines 365–394 and 850–873, test/sim_grid/out/bootstrap_coverage.csv, and code/KCOR.py lines 4397–4408.

5. **The Czech provenance and diagnostic gate need reconciliation.** The supplement says every age band passed. Its example for 2021_24, birth-year group 1950, dose 0 gives scale 0.00429, variance 0.802, RMSE 0.00337 and 97 observations. All four numbers match the *version 6 negative-control* log, where RMSE is measured on cumulative hazard. The archived Czech primary log for that same key gives scale 0.000588, variance 8.7, 41 points and a clamped negative offset. It also records insufficient signal for several cohorts. Production paths return success and zero variance for inadequate deaths or a nonpositive first-pass offset and can construct output even after fit failure. A well-estimated zero and inadequate information require different treatment. Also, the Czech configuration comments that 2023 showed the least vaccine harm, so the claimed independence of window choice from outcomes needs a dated source or an independently tested window. Rebuild every table and figure from a pinned run with its exact settings, cohort keys, gates and interval method. Publish rejected rows, profile bounds and reasons. See supplement lines 263–277, 366–378 and 398–421, test/negative_control/out/KCOR_summary.log lines 2 and 48, data/Czech/KCOR_summary.log lines 214–245, data/Czech/Czech.yaml lines 22–32, and code/KCOR.py lines 1253–1275, 1486–1506 and 8033–8047.

6. **The optional wave correction uses the wrong survivor moment.** Prior to a wave, gamma frailty with baseline cumulative hazard $B$ gives surviving frailty shape $1/\theta$ and scale $s=\theta/(1+\theta B)$. Its mean is $1/(1+\theta B)$, and its variance is $\theta/(1+\theta B)^2$. The code passes $s$ as the variance of a *new mean-one gamma* and computes its moment. For example, with $\theta=B=1$ and exponent 2, the true survivor second moment is 0.5 while the code returns 1.5. During a wave whose individual hazard contains a term proportional to $z^\alpha$, the survivor density acquires the additional factor $\exp(-A_{\rm cum}z^\alpha)$ and generally leaves the gamma family. Dividing the entire accumulated excess by a time-varying endpoint moment also differs from correcting each increment and integrating. The paper calls the weakly identified Czech module inactive, but the archived run headed “Primary Analysis” forces 1.18 and logs nonzero corrections. Release the primary results with this module off. For a future extension, integrate survival over the joint baseline-plus-wave model, simulate individual-level effects and propagate uncertainty in any external effect scale. See paper lines 508–547, 568–598 and 876–898, code/KCOR.py lines 1028–1047 and 2591–2635, data/Czech/Czech.yaml lines 14–20, and data/Czech/KCOR_summary.log lines 5, 24 and 277–279.

7. **The controls support narrower conclusions.** Assigning pseudo-dose labels by age removes a treatment interpretation but leaves genuine age differences. Anchoring divides away *any* constant cumulative-hazard ratio, including a persistent proportional treatment effect. Two reported pseudo-control intervals, 1.055–1.097 and 1.070–1.112, exclude unity. The production contamination experiment is informative precisely because it finds departures in both directions. Its perturbation adds actual death risk to one arm, so absence of a treatment effect does not imply equality of all-cause risk; the experiment should distinguish the uncontaminated target from the changed population outcome. Its one design with 20 replicates per cell cannot bound confounding in the Czech data. Present raw and anchored curves, prespecify a practically meaningful null tolerance, and tabulate both false acceptance and bias among accepted fits across alternative slopes, shock timing, crossover and frailty distributions. Withdraw blanket claims that violations or overlap are conservative. See paper lines 164–169, 791–809, 842–854, 1023–1045 and 1228–1237, and test/quiet_window_contamination/run_contamination_test.py lines 151–175.

The first result I would want from a revision is a blinded, repeated-sampling comparison of the known-variance and estimated-variance versions on identical generated counts, followed by a reproducible Czech run using the tested estimator. Until then, the evidence supports a conditional transformation of cohort mortality curves. The stronger empirical claim that this procedure reliably identifies and removes real-world selection from those curves remains open.
