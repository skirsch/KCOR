# Independent review of KCOR

The proportional gamma inversion and the fixed-slope identification proposition in §§2.4.2–2.4.4 are sound under their stated assumptions. The working model defines a descriptive contrast worth studying. I found errors in the construction and reporting of the Czech example, two simulation comparisons, and two numerical safeguards. These have different consequences for the central method.

## 1. The all-ages fit and its mortality trajectory use different people

The ordinary path in `code/KCOR.py` excludes the aggregate row from the per-age fit (lines 6866–6868), then fits the all-ages parameters to the sum over strictly positive birth years (lines 7173–7184). For hazard accumulation it retains the existing all-ages row if present (lines 7705–7709), and uses that trajectory in the all-ages report (lines 5149–5166). The converter's full-population aggregate includes unknown birth year. Applying the parameters fitted to the known-age subset to the complete observed trajectory violates the stated within-cohort estimator, even when the gamma model holds. Bootstrap reconstruction of both groups from the same sampled people preserves this mismatch.

In the supplied `data/Czech/KCOR_CMR.xlsx`, sheet `2021_24`, the dose-0 opening risk set on the aggregate row is 5,861,036. Known years contribute 4,273,870 and unknown years contribute 1,587,166. Their opening death counts are 911 and 119. With the four configured Czech windows, two skipped weeks, four anchors, slope 0.085/52, and the module off, the supplied fitter gives dose-0 theta 31.384 from known years and 31.065 from the full aggregate. The corresponding dose-2 opening risk set, 1,838,302, is entirely known-age. The example has a modest difference in fitted theta despite a 27% membership mismatch for dose 0. Its size in another dataset depends on the distribution of missing ages. A workbook containing only the all-ages row also has no positive-year rows for this fit branch.

**Fix.** Fit the aggregate using the same date-specific deaths and opening counts that enter its inversion. Check the supplied all-ages row against its component histories when both are present. Use one aggregate definition for point estimates, diagnostics, and resampling. Exercise the normal workbook path with unknown ages, a restricted year range and an aggregate-only input.

## 2. The Czech cohort excludes people using post-enrollment vaccine records

Before any enrollment is constructed, `code/KCOR_CMR.py:477–495` inspects the manufacturer's code for each person's first four doses over their whole record and drops anyone with a recognized non-mRNA or other non-mRNA-coded dose. Only afterward does the converter freeze dose at enrollment. Two people with identical histories at enrollment can therefore be included differently depending on whether one survives to receive a later non-mRNA dose. In particular, someone who dies before receiving that later dose can remain in the dose-0 denominator while a similar survivor is removed retrospectively. Fixed group membership after enrollment does not prevent this form of selection.

I counted the affected records in the supplied full compressed registry using the converter's infection-row, birth-band and survival-at-entry restrictions and manufacturer code mapping. At 2021-24, 363,037 otherwise eligible people without an earlier disqualifying dose had such a dose dated in the enrollment week or later. Of these, 354,608 had none of the first four doses before the enrollment week. The calculation treats all vaccinations during the enrollment week as subsequent, consistent with the converter's start-of-week dose rule. This count establishes that the mechanism operates in these data. It does not quantify how much bias it causes in a particular KCOR ratio.

**Fix.** For an enrollment-state comparison, apply any product eligibility restriction only to doses recorded before enrollment. Compare the resulting counts and estimates with the existing extract. If the future-product restriction defines the intended descriptive population, name it in the Czech methods and tables and disclose its magnitude and resulting survivor selection. The generic sentence on possible post-enrollment eligibility in §2.2 does not tell the reader which records were excluded.

## 3. The supplement's raw Czech numbers and all-pass statement cannot be reproduced as presented

SI §S6.1.2 gives all-ages cumulative hazards of 0.023160 and 0.073323, ratio 0.3159, from enrollment 2021-24 through 2024-16. Summing the paper's own weekly transform over all 149 inclusive weeks on the supplied all-ages rows gives **0.0202043, 0.0740106 and 0.272992** for doses 0 and 2 and their ratio. Both `data/Czech/KCOR_CMR.xlsx` and the named input used in `analysis/quiet_windows/scan.py` give those same counts. Both span dates after the stated endpoint. The current full-aggregate dose-0 theta of 31.065 in finding 1 also differs substantially from the supplement's 4.98. An older extract, age restriction or fitter could explain the discrepancy, but the table does not identify that configuration.

The 78/78 figure in SI §S5.4 is a count of numerical acceptances. In `analysis/quiet_windows/scan.py:85`, passing requires solver success, status “ok” and no degenerate-parameter flag. That condition does not test the required window-omission stability, quiet-curve shape, or residual time structure listed in §S2. With the production four-window fit I obtained “ok” and a flagged count residual simultaneously for both full aggregate doses and for the 1940s-birth dose-2 group (minimum doubled tail probability about 4.1 × 10⁻⁹ for the latter). This is an exploratory flag, so it does not itself establish lack of identification. It demonstrates why numerical acceptance cannot certify every diagnostic. The all-“Yes” table in §S6.1.2 needs its own evidence.

**Fix.** Regenerate each SI table from an explicitly identified extract, inclusion rule, age mapping, time range and estimator. Archive the generating command and numeric columns. Report numerical acceptance separately from the listed diagnostic checks, and show which checks were evaluated for each group. Give the historical label and configuration to any values retained only as historical illustrations.

## 4. The roughly 5,000-per-arm precision claim contradicts its simulation

Section 5.3 says smaller cohorts had appreciable variability and occasional spurious deviations, while approximately 5,000 per arm yielded narrow uncertainty. `test/sim_grid/code/generate_cohort_size_sim.py` changes a seed on each replicate, but the gamma-null generator in `generate_sim_grid.py:383–392` sets deaths to the deterministic expectation, with fractional risk sets. The saved `test/sim_grid/out/cohort_size_sensitivity.csv` gives **the same median 1.007110465349759, zero 95% width, and zero false-signal rate** for all six sizes from 1,000 through 50,000 and all 500 replicates. Increasing population size simply rescales both counts. These data cannot establish either half of the stated comparison.

**Fix.** Remove the size claim, or draw independent event histories with the specified cohort size, refit the current estimator for each replicate, and report endpoint error and interval width at the chosen horizon, the number of deaths, and the fraction of unavailable estimates. The more recent information and population-size experiments answer different questions and should keep their own designs and labels.

## 5. The time-varying Cox comparator discards the at-risk survivors

In `test/sim_grid/code/generate_sim_grid.py`, `fit_time_varying_cox` creates one terminal record per represented person, then selects only records whose **terminal time** lies in each interval before fitting PHReg (roughly lines 745–767). Someone who survives beyond week 20 is at risk during weeks 0–19, but is absent from the first fit. Administrative survivors at the overall horizon are excluded even from the final interval by the strict upper bound. The fit consequently compares event timing among selected failures. The constructed time-by-cohort column is unused. Also, truncating every week's fractional deaths to integers independently does not preserve the original risk histories. The saved `comparison_table.csv` and the manuscript's comparison-of-estimands table use the resulting summaries. This defect is specific to the historical time-window comparator, not the separate individual-record Cox exercise in §2.11.1.

There is a distinct target issue even with a valid comparator. Equal treatment hazards at fixed frailty and different within-cohort frailty variances genuinely produce different marginal survival and RMST. A departure of an accurately estimated marginal RMST ratio from one therefore does not establish bias for RMST's own target. The comparison code sets its truth to one and calls the departure “bias”; it assigns “Low (stable trajectory)” to KCOR whenever its median is finite and “Moderate” to RMST without estimating sampling variation. The frailty-grid KCOR exercise supplies the true theta to the inversion, as the text correctly discloses. Its comparison cannot establish superior performance of the fitted estimator.

**Fix.** Include everyone at risk at the start of each interval, censor at its end, and fit interval-specific coefficients or a single correctly specified counting-process Cox model. Simulate coherent individual histories or use the original risk-set counts directly. Benchmark each method against its population estimand under the generator, and measure variation across independent datasets. Label the oracle inversion as such in comparative conclusions.

## 6. Active baseline-rate bounds and nonlinear quadrature can invalidate successful fits

The core least-squares objective in §2.5 allows any positive baseline rate, but the code bounds it to [10⁻⁸, 0.1] per week. Both proportional and nonlinear fits check for an active upper theta bound while omitting an analogous k-bound check. In 52 noiseless quiet weeks generated by the supplied exact weekly hazard with k = 0.2, theta = 2 and gamma = 0.085/52, the production fitter returned k = 0.1, theta = 1.41361 and status “ok”. This is relevant if the method is used for high weekly event rates. It is not an explanation for the Czech mortality results. **Fix.** Allow the parameter range declared in the methods or document a domain restriction and flag either active rate bound. Report the constrained fit distinctly from an interior solution.

The optional nonlinear fitting routine uses `Survival.hazard` in `code/kcor_survivor.py`. Its fast quadrature with moment corrections switches to a different formula at a hard cutoff. In a targeted admissible test with theta = 99, alpha = 1.18 and B = 0.01, increasing W from 2.1270330577 × (1 − 10⁻⁹) to 2.1270330577 × (1 + 10⁻⁹) **decreases** the computed cumulative hazard from 0.05372711 to 0.05211058. True cumulative hazard must be increasing in W. The scalar exposure solver assumes monotonicity. A separate check with theta = 99, alpha = 1.30, B = 0.01 and W = 1 gave 0.04435512 in the fitter, versus 0.04528939 by independent integration of the gamma survival loss and by the correction routine's quadrature. The issue is demonstrated in the high-frailty regime, not established in the reported primary analyses. **Fix.** Use a common accuracy-controlled evaluation for fit and inversion, compare to independent quadrature at high frailty and test monotonicity across numerical branch changes.

## 7. An observed excess-hazard contrast needs a definition consistent with the nonlinear survivor model

Sections 2.7.1–2.7.2 write the wave component as A(t) times the moment of Z to the power alpha among actual survivors. That instantaneous component formula is valid. An observed total hazard minus a projected no-wave marginal hazard is a different quantity once the wave changes which people survive. The subtraction adds the term b(t) times the difference between mean frailty in actual survivors and mean frailty in no-wave survivors. Subtracting the dose-zero group's total hazard, as the Czech illustration does, adds another cohort's baseline and wave terms. The paper acknowledges the Czech proxy in §3.4.2 but still presents the general ratio as an operational localization equation for observed excess. A common amplitude also need not cancel from a ratio of **weekly integrated** components when the survivor moments vary differently within the week.

For a simple exact example, take mean-one exponential frailty, alpha = 1, previous B = W = 1, and current baseline and wave rates both 0.1. Actual survivors have mean 1/3 and no-wave survivors mean 1/2. The wave component is 0.1/3, but observed total minus projected no-wave total is 0.2/3 − 0.1/2 = 1/60, only half as large. This issue does not invalidate the conditional survival inversion for a **supplied** alpha. The Czech primary analysis already leaves the module off.

**Fix.** Define the excess used for localization conditional on the same actual survivor state, and fit or test the shared intensity and external dose scales with the integrated weekly survival equations. Verify recovery with this full data-generating law and independent counts. Otherwise confine the proposed moment-ratio approach to data where the appropriate instantaneous component is measured or an approximation has been justified.

## Interpretation

The fact that aggregate mortality cannot distinguish every alternative source of curvature is an acknowledged identification limitation. It does not invalidate the conditional gamma identity. There is meaningful positive evidence in the matched-model survivor simulations, and explicit reporting of the Czech exponent's non-identification and sensitivity to window selection is appropriate. The immediate priorities are matching eligibility and all-ages populations, correcting the unsupported empirical and simulation claims, and repairing the comparator. None requires abandoning the descriptive estimand.
