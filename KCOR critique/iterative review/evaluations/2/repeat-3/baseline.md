# Review of KCOR

**Major revision.** KCOR has a valid mathematical core. If individual hazards factor into a common time path within each cohort times a fixed mean-one gamma frailty of known variance, the gamma Laplace transform and its inverse in §2.4.2 are correct. With an independently justified Gompertz slope, sufficiently informative quiet-period counts can estimate a variance *within that model*. The manuscript goes further in attributing observed curvature to depletion, promising that failures will be visible, and presenting validation of a pipeline that differs from the one tested. I would retain an explicitly model-dependent descriptive contrast and revise the following points before interpreting registry results.

## Identification and interpretation

1. **The paper’s scaling invariance is false as stated.** Put \(G(t)=(e^{\gamma t}-1)/\gamma\) and \(H(k,\theta)=\log(1+\theta kG)/\theta\). Scaling the **observed** hazard by \(c\) gives exactly \(cH(k,\theta)=H(ck,\theta/c)\). Even if both cohorts experience the same \(c\), each fitted variance changes by \(1/c\). The corresponding transformed baseline ratio can cancel a common scale, which is a different claim. Multiplying the **individual baseline** by \(c\) leaves the true frailty variance unchanged, but the resulting marginal hazard is generally not \(c\) times its former value. State which intervention acts on which scale and drop the claim that heterogeneous effects are generically second order. See the abstract and §§1.5, 2.4.4.

2. **Replace the curvature proposition with its actual identification argument.** In an undisturbed continuous-time Gompertz–gamma window the observed rate satisfies \(h'=\gamma h-\theta h^2\). With fixed known \(\gamma\), positive \(h\), and an exact rate trajectory, \(\theta=(\gamma h-h')/h^2\). At \(\theta=0\) the cumulative hazard can be curved. At \(\theta k=\gamma>0\) the observed hazard is constant and the cumulative hazard straight. Thus nonzero curvature and positive frailty are not equivalent. At low event rates a small error in the assumed slope has effect \(\delta\gamma/h\) in this local calculation. Supply a grouped-data analogue, joint parameter profiles and slope sensitivity for the actual cohorts. Assess linearity against Gompertz time \(G(t)\), or examine predicted count residuals. A correct Gompertz cumulative baseline is curved in calendar time. See §§2.4.3–2.4.4 and SI §§S2, S5.

3. **Perfect fit cannot identify the source of curvature.** For any nondecreasing observed \(H\) and any proposed \(q>0\), \(B_q=(e^{qH}-1)/q\) with gamma variance \(q\) generates precisely that observed survival. A homogeneous population with time-varying individual baseline \(H\) does too. A cohort-specific change in baseline risk or uptake-related health can therefore mimic depletion across every check constructed from the same counts. A constant exposure effect is also inseparable from the cohort baseline scale. This does not invalidate the parametric fit. It invalidates assurances of conservative failure and unique interpretation of residuals in §§5.1–5.2 and SI §S6. Show alternative baseline/frailty models compatible with the data and their KCOR ranges. Add independent information on baseline drift, uptake and comorbidity where available. Treat the skip as a survivor landmark. Report losses and risks before it, since a contrast after the skip excludes early events.

## Estimator and reproducibility

4. **Implement one estimator and use it throughout.** Section 2.5 specifies joint seed estimation of \(k,\theta\) in the first quiet window and refitting over quiet bins alone. Production fixes \(k\) to the mean detrended observed hazard over the first post-skip anchor bins, fits only \(\theta\) in the first quiet interval, and includes anchor bins in the final objective even if outside every quiet window. Early depletion or early risk changes therefore enter the fit directly. The gap offset also reads the *start* of the last gap bin, omitting that bin’s increment when the next quiet period begins. The Euler reconstruction used for offsets differs from the exact inverse used for final output. For observed integrated increment \(u\), the exact increment in \(B\) is \((1+\theta B_{\rm start})\operatorname{expm1}(\theta u)/\theta\). Use consistent bin boundaries and exact increments, fit expected interval counts with the correct risk sets, and test round trips with a shock wholly inside the last gap bin. See §2.5 and **code/KCOR.py** lines 1225–1242, 1336–1383, 1450–1477 and 1534–1549.

5. **Make failure and sensitivity settings visible in the reported result.** A nonpositive first-pass offset can yield an insufficient-signal result with numerical \(\theta=0\) and optimizer success true. Sparse cohorts have a similar fallback. Numerically this passes an unadjusted hazard into the ratio. A genuine zero estimate, a failed fit and a sensitivity run need distinct output states. The Czech configuration explicitly forces \(\alpha=1.18\) and also fills the identified-alpha field, whereas §3.4 reports empirical non-identification. Its comment says 2023 was chosen because it showed the least vaccine harm. This precludes treating the file itself as evidence of outcome-blind prespecification. Ship a primary configuration with the optional module inactive, separate labeled sensitivity configurations, a dated window-selection rule, and a table mapping every reported result to its configuration, fit status and uncertainty method. See **data/Czech/Czech.yaml** lines 14–40, **code/KCOR.py** lines 1254–1278 and 1486–1514, and SI §§S2, S6. The “representative” illustration also selects final KCOR between 0.85 and 1.15 and favors reduced curvature. Disclose that selection in its caption or choose independently of the result (**code/generate_kcor_empirical_intuition_figure.py**, lines 101–145).

6. **Separate the raw ratio, anchored ratio and observed risks.** Section 2.1.1 divides by one time-point ratio, §2.8 describes division by a window mean, and the production output called KCOR divides by one indexed ratio with a silent fallback to 1 (**code/KCOR.py**, lines 4348–4366). Anchoring maps *any* constant cohort ratio to one, including a genuine persistent level difference. Different-age pseudo-dose cohorts lack a treatment-label effect but can have genuinely different baseline cumulative hazards. A flat anchored control addresses relative drift only. Moreover, the reported 2022_06 empirical controls include 1.0756 [1.055, 1.097] and 1.0911 [1.070, 1.112]. State the target and a prespecified equivalence margin for each control. Publish unanchored and anchored outputs, observed mortality and age-standardized contrasts side by side. In SI §S6 the pooled raw ratio is 0.316 while every displayed age-specific ratio exceeds one. Baseline composition matters before any inversion. The unit-frailty cumulative hazard is a model baseline, and a cumulative hazard ratio is not a ratio of mortality probabilities.

## Validation and uncertainty

7. **Several validation results answer easier questions.** Section 2.11 says the Cox comparison estimates frailty from aggregate data. Its generator explicitly passes the simulated true variance to KCOR (**test/sim_grid/code/generate_cox_bias_sim.py**, lines 209–235, 278–285). The multiwave recovery test uses a noiseless recurrence and takes \(k=h(0)\) exactly (**test/theta0/theta0_nwave.py**, lines 96–106, 182–195). The simulation grid uses an earlier single-window cumulative-hazard fit (**test/sim_grid/code/generate_sim_grid.py**, lines 850–875). These are checks of algebra or historical methods. Run the production estimator blind to truth on repeated individual-level or exact grouped-event simulations, and report oracle, estimated and failed-fit results separately. Compare each method to its own target. Different frailty mixtures can produce real marginal HR and RMST differences under a null individual intervention effect. The plotted shared-frailty reference is an explicitly calibrated proxy and supplies no independent comparison of fitted models.

8. **The coverage study cannot support the reported uncertainty procedure.** In the committed 500-dataset, 200-bootstrap run I counted exactly one distinct outer point estimate per 500 rows in each of the gamma-null, harm, benefit and sparse scenarios. The gamma generator uses fractional expected deaths deterministically. “Truth” is the same estimator applied to a reference draw. Those four scenarios report 100% coverage, and the non-gamma scenario reports 25.4% against one sampled reference estimate. The paper’s table still says a full run is pending. The coverage driver independently Poisson-resamples each week’s observed deaths and caps by remaining survivors. Section 2.9 instead specifies contiguous blocks, and the production comparison code calculates clipped analytic log intervals. These procedures cannot validate one another. Simulate independent stochastic cohorts, derive the population target independently, use the same prespecified estimation and interval pipeline as the empirical analysis, and report bias, coverage, Monte Carlo error, failure frequency and coverage conditional on passing. With one event per person and fixed follow-up, a multinomial resample over death weeks plus final survivors preserves risk-set accounting without moving calendar waves. Accommodate overlap when pooling repeated enrollments. See **test/sim_grid/out/bootstrap_coverage{,_replicates}.csv**, **test/sim_grid/code/compute_bootstrap_coverage.py** lines 100–123 and 201–229, **generate_sim_grid.py** lines 367–394, and **code/KCOR.py** lines 4377–4408.

9. **The contamination experiment does not establish a Czech bias bound.** Section 3.3.2 applies a positive, quiet-window-only additive excess to cohort A. The code includes that component in actual deaths while accumulating only the baseline in the simulated latent frailty path. Once extra deaths occur, the hidden treatment null, the baseline-only contrast and the observed all-cause contrast are distinct. Even at zero contamination the archived comorbidity sweep averages 1.034 with 75% of replicates above one, versus 1.062 and 65% above one at the claimed 66%-of-baseline threshold. Exceeding one under a null is not a sign reversal or a false-positive test. The dynamic sweep tests one sign and time course; a real deferral mechanism can suppress early risk. Compare paired contaminated and uncontaminated estimates on a specified target, vary sign, cohort, persistence and onset, and report intervals and diagnostic failures. Calibrate plausible magnitudes against independent observations before generalizing to Czech mortality. See **test/quiet_window_contamination/run_contamination_test.py** lines 151–175, 253–286 and 294–320, and its **out/quiet_contamination_agg.csv**.

## Optional epidemic-wave model

10. **The survivor moment and cumulative correction require a new derivation.** Under proportional gamma selection to baseline cumulative hazard \(B\), surviving frailty has shape \(1/\theta\) and scale \(\theta/(1+\theta B)\). Hence \(E[Z^\alpha\mid T>t]=\Gamma(1/\theta+\alpha)\{\theta/(1+\theta B)\}^\alpha/\Gamma(1/\theta)\). In particular at \(\alpha=1\) it is \(1/(1+\theta B)\). Production instead plugs a declining parameter into a fresh *mean-one* gamma moment and sets the moment to one for \(\alpha=1\) (**code/KCOR.py**, lines 1028–1048 and 2557–2596). Inactivation at one is sensible for an adjustment *relative to* proportional frailty, but that requires a different definition of the factor. More fundamentally, a wave hazard \(a(t)Z^\alpha\) tilts the surviving density by \(\exp[-BZ-AZ^\alpha]\), which is generally no longer gamma for \(\alpha\ne1\). The core inverse then already misrepresents wave selection. Even on a scale where instantaneous excess equals \(a(t)F(t)\), dividing accumulated excess by today’s \(F(t)\) cannot recover \(\int a(s)\,ds\) unless the factor is constant or additional restrictions hold. Positive-only rescaling and a cumulative maximum do not supply these missing identities. Keep the module exploratory until a joint survival model propagating the survivor distribution and interval-specific excess recovers independently simulated truth. Propagate uncertainty in the external effect scale as well. See §§2.7.1–2.7.3, SI §S2.1 and **code/KCOR.py** lines 2594–2645.

The core transformation is worth keeping as a sensitivity analysis: show how much a specified, plausible degree of depletion would change an observed comparison. Estimating that degree from the same curves, validating it, and attributing a remaining difference to vaccination are three different tasks. The current edition establishes the first task under restrictive assumptions and leaves the latter two unresolved. This review used the supplied repository edition, its manuscript sources, implementation and archived results. I did not independently regenerate the registry analysis.
